As Digital Technologies teachers, we often think about algorithms in terms of lines of code or logic flowcharts on screens. However, when working with Year 7 students—and especially without access to computers—unplugged coding activities can be some of the most effective ways to introduce core algorithmic concepts. They make abstract ideas tangible and foster collaboration and problem-solving skills. Plus, unplugged lessons are incredibly useful in classrooms where device access is limited or when you want to switch up the pace.

In my experience teaching algorithms to Year 7s, the key is to focus on clear, concrete experiences that mimic the way algorithms work: step-by-step instructions, decision points, and sequencing. Below are three activities I’ve used with great success, which you can try tomorrow—no screen required.

### 1. **Human Robot**

This classic unplugged activity teaches students to write and follow precise instructions, which is the essence of an algorithm.

**How it works:**  
- Pair students up. One is the "programmer," and the other the "robot."  
- The programmer writes down a step-by-step set of instructions to navigate the “robot” from one point in the classroom to another (e.g., walk forward 3 steps, turn right 90 degrees, take 2 steps, etc.).  
- The robot can only follow exact instructions—no guessing allowed!  
- After the first trial, pairs swap roles and try again.

This demonstrates how important clarity and order are in algorithms. It also naturally leads to discussions on debugging when the robot ends up off-course.

### 2. **Paper Airplane Algorithm**

Creating a paper airplane is a familiar task for students, and breaking it down into algorithmic steps reinforces the idea that an algorithm is a precise procedure.

**How it works:**  
- Ask students to work individually or in small groups to write a set of step-by-step instructions for making a simple paper airplane.  
- Once instructions are written, have students exchange their algorithms with a partner who follows the instructions exactly to fold their own plane.  
- Test the planes by flying them. Often, mistakes in the instructions become obvious when the plane doesn’t fly well or folds incorrectly.  
- Reflect on where the algorithms were unclear and how they can be improved.

This exercise emphasizes that algorithms must be detailed and unambiguous, and highlights the iterative process of refining an algorithm.

### 3. **Algorithm Sorting Challenge**

Sorting is a foundational algorithmic concept. You can teach basic sorting without computers using physical objects.

**How it works:**  
- Provide students with a mixed collection of cards or objects (e.g., numbered cards or coloured blocks).  
- Give them the task to sort the items in ascending order—but specify a simple “sorting algorithm” for them to follow, such as bubble sort: repeatedly compare adjacent items and swap if out of order.  
- Students apply the steps physically, swapping items until everything is sorted.  
- To deepen understanding, after running through bubble sort, compare with other methods like selection sort, tested physically again.

This hands-on version of sorting algorithms helps students internalize the mechanics behind automated sorting, a core concept in programming.

---

Through these activities, Year 7 students develop a more intuitive understanding of algorithms—not as abstract lines of code, but as logical sequences of steps that can be precisely defined, tested, and improved. It also encourages collaboration and critical thinking, two essential skills for budding coders.

While unplugged activities are fantastic starters, they also build strong foundations for when students do move on to actual coding environments. In fact, I’ve found that students who grasp these concepts comfortably without screens show greater confidence when they later work with programming languages.
